# Froyo splits into two diverging markets

Frozen yogurt in the United States is not one story but two running in parallel: the legacy self-serve retail category — the pay-by-weight, toppings-bar format that defined Pinkberry- and TCBY-era froyo — is still shrinking, with Circana scan data showing dollar sales down **5% to $318 million** and unit sales down **3% to 72 million** in a recent 52-week 2024 read, even as overall ice cream/sherbet sales grew 3% in the same window ([DairyFoods.com](https://www.dairyfoods.com/articles/97734-2024-state-of-the-dairy-industry-ice-cream)). At the same time, a visible, traffic-data-backed revival is underway: Placer.ai recorded frozen yogurt foot traffic growing faster than the broader dessert category in 15 of 23 months from January 2024, with year-over-year visit growth topping **+18%** in parts of H1 2026 while traditional ice cream shop traffic fell as low as **-8.5%** YoY in the same stretch ([Placer.ai](https://www.placer.ai/anchor/articles/frozen-yogurts-comeback-is-more-than-nostalgia)). The reconciling fact is that the revival is happening in a *different format* than the decline: it is counter-service, Greek-yogurt-based, protein-forward, and premium-priced, led by independent boutiques (Myka, Mikono, Birdie's, Fira) and a handful of modernizing chains, not a return to the 20-flavor self-serve wall. For Yogurtland, this means the strategic question is not "is froyo growing or dying" — it's whether to defend the self-serve format that built the brand or follow the counter-service, premium, protein-forward model that is actually capturing Gen Z attention and foot traffic today. Layered on top, South Korea's Yoajung has turned the same base product into the world's largest froyo chain (684 units, +120 in 2025 alone) and is actively exporting that model into the US and seven other countries, while GLP-1 drugs are reshaping frozen-treat economics broadly in ways that favor smaller, higher-protein, premium portions over volume.

## US retail scan data and foot traffic tell contradictory stories

The headline numbers on US frozen yogurt depend entirely on which data source is asked, and the discrepancy itself is the first strategic insight. IBISWorld-sourced industry tracking shows frozen yogurt store revenue falling from **$826.1 million (2020) to $802.5 million (2021) to $654 million (2022)**, continuing a decline that began after the category's 2009–2013 boom, when it grew at an annualized **22.7%** before contracting at **-5.6%** annualized from 2013–2018 ([Guidant Financial](https://www.guidantfinancial.com/blog/frozen-yogurt-infographic/); [Restaurant Business Online](https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/financing/second-frozen-yogurt-craze-continues-its-long-decline)). TCBY alone fell from roughly 3,000 peak locations to about 123–168 domestic outlets depending on the count year, an **~88% collapse** ([The Daily Meal](https://www.thedailymeal.com/1806750/rise-fall-tcby/)), and Red Mango, Cherry Berry, and Orange Leaf are each down 60–80% or more from their peaks, with Orange Leaf still closing long-running units as recently as December 2025 and January 2026 ([Guidant Financial](https://www.guidantfinancial.com/blog/2019s-top-5-frozen-yogurt-franchise-brands/); [TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/restaurants/orange-leaf-popular-frozen-yogurt-chain-closes-most-locations)). Forward-looking forecasters tell a different story only because they define the category differently: Mordor Intelligence projects a **3.69% CAGR** for the US market 2025–2030, and other firms size the US market at roughly **$0.92–0.97 billion**, figures that likely blend a more stable grocery/packaged-retail segment into a shrinking specialty-shop segment ([Mordor Intelligence](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/united-states-frozen-yogurt-market); [Fortune Business Insights](https://www.fortunebusinessinsights.com/industry-reports/frozen-yogurt-market-100799)). Globally, estimates for the current period range from **$1.9 billion to over $10 billion** depending on the research vendor — a spread wide enough that any single absolute dollar figure should be treated as directional, not authoritative ([search-aggregated, multiple vendors]).

What is not in dispute is the unit-level bifurcation happening right now. The world's top 10 froyo chains grew from roughly 1,956 locations (end of 2023) to 2,267 (2024, +16%) to about 2,821 (2025, +24% YoY) — but that growth is dominated by non-US brands, led by South Korea's Yoajung at 684 units and Spain's Llaollao at 317+ ([International Frozen Yogurt Association](https://internationalfrozenyogurt.com/news/the-froyo-year-in-review-2025-edition/)). Within the US specifically, legacy self-serve chains were still net closers in 2024 — Menchie's fell to 316 locations (-10), Pinkberry to 191 (-62), TCBY to roughly 123–125 (-16) — while newer-format entrants expanded briskly off smaller bases: 16 Handles grew toward 67 total units with per-store sales rising from **$660,000 (2022) to $805,000 (2024)**, and Yogurtland — closed ~60 US stores during the pandemic, now ~200 locations, 80% in California — signed 13 new store agreements in December 2025 and projects 22 openings in 2026, its first expansion into Idaho ([IFYA](https://internationalfrozenyogurt.com/news/the-year-in-froyo-review-2024-edition/); [ICSC](https://www.icsc.com/news-and-views/icsc-exchange/froyo-chains-are-expanding-again); [PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/surge-in-froyo-demand-drives-16-handles-coast-to-coast-expansion-and-strong-2025-sales-momentum-302655913.html)). The US led the world in new froyo business openings in 2025 with **100 new openings** across the country, ahead of the Philippines (99), Australia (74), and Indonesia (49) — evidence the American comeback is real at the entrepreneurial/independent level even as the old chain roster keeps contracting ([IFYA](https://internationalfrozenyogurt.com/news/the-froyo-year-in-review-2025-edition/)).

## Health positioning is bifurcating into real science and soft marketing

Protein has become the one health claim in frozen yogurt that is both commercially central and operationally constrained. Roughly **44% of new froyo product launches now include probiotic claims and 37% feature reduced-sugar formulations**, and about half of 18–30-year-olds say they choose frozen yogurt specifically for its lower fat and higher protein content ([MarketsandMarkets](https://www.marketsandmarkets.com/blog/FB/Protein-Yogurt)). But 16 Handles CEO Neil Hershman — whose brand has leaned hardest into the trend — is candid about the limits: "it's almost impossible to run really high-protein whey powder through pasteurization," meaning high-protein froyo claims face genuine manufacturing constraints, not just marketing choice ([Modern Retail](https://www.modernretail.co/marketing/whats-driving-frozen-yogurts-second-act-as-16-handles-and-go-greek-experience-a-renaissance/)). Gut-health and probiotic claims sit on softer ground: independent health commentary notes frozen yogurt isn't held to the same regulatory standard as refrigerated dairy yogurt, many frozen products "contain no live cultures at all," and froyo "often delivers far fewer probiotics than traditional, refrigerated yogurt" ([Cymbiotika](https://cymbiotika.com/blogs/health-hub/does-frozen-yogurt-still-have-probiotics-exploring-the-truth-behind-this-popular-treat)). This gap between credible protein science and softer gut-health signaling is a real brand-risk lever: a marketing team overstating probiotic benefits invites credibility challenges that a protein claim, properly caveated, would not.

Simultaneously, part of the category is explicitly retreating from health framing altogether. Trend journalism describes shops "swapping diet culture for indulgence," where froyo "once read as a 'better-for-you' dessert" but "now feels like an everyday treat" ([Time Out](https://www.timeout.com/usa/news/the-frozen-yogurt-revival-is-officialand-the-fro-yo-is-better-than-you-remember-020426)), and Mintel's category-wide finding for US ice cream broadly is that **"indulgence tops health"** even as its "Future of Ice Cream 2025" report separately flags "fro-yo will gain new purpose" as a named trend ([Mintel](https://www.mintel.com/press-centre/indulgence-tops-health-for-us-ice-cream/)). Plant-based demand follows a similar split: the global dairy-free soft-serve-mix market is forecast to grow from **$1.1 billion (2024) to $2.7 billion by 2033** (10.3% CAGR), with North America holding ~38% of volume and Asia-Pacific the fastest-growing region at a projected 13.1% CAGR ([MarketIntelo](https://marketintelo.com/report/frozen-dairy-free-soft-serve-mix-market)) — but this strength shows up almost entirely in B2B soft-serve-base supply rather than in any named chain's same-store sales data, and Mintel's parallel finding that "plant-based ice creams are failing to gain further ground" in the broader frozen-dessert category is a useful caution against assuming dairy-free is an automatic growth lever for froyo specifically. Premiumization, by contrast, is the best-evidenced and least ambiguous trend in the category: Pinkberry commands a reported **15–20% price premium** on a "9.1/10 Premium Brand Equity" score ([VerifiedMarketResearch](https://www.verifiedmarketresearch.com/blog/top-frozen-yogurt-companies/)), and American consumers are reportedly willing to spend **up to $20** on a froyo serving topped with pistachio cream, cold-pressed olive oil, Maldon sea salt, or wildflower honey — toppings now associated with independent shops like Birdie's, Mimi's, and Nashville's Culture Club rather than the old self-serve bar ([Fox News/AOL](https://www.aol.com/articles/20-frozen-yogurt-protein-packed-120025000.html)).

## Gen Z revives the category but rejects its original format

The clearest, most consistently sourced finding across the research is that Gen Z nostalgia is driving a genuine froyo comeback — but for a format that pointedly is not self-serve. Coverage frames it directly: "a decade after the self-serve boom of the late 2000s and early 2010s, froyo is once again drawing lines, from New York City's West Village to San Francisco's Mission," but the shops going viral "aren't the ones with 20 different flavors and a self-serve toppings bar" — instead, smaller portions and counter service define the moment, with "gourmet, sundae-ish creations" driving social virality ([search-aggregated SF Standard/Axios reporting](https://sfstandard.com/2026/08/09/sf-frozen-yogurt-trend/)). Salon frames it culturally as "Obama-era nostalgia," and Modern Retail notes Gen Z's "viral experiential shops" combine wellness interest (gut health, protein) with aesthetics to rebrand froyo "from a nostalgic childhood memory into a trendy, modern lifestyle hub" ([Salon](https://www.salon.com/2025/06/25/to-gen-z-obama-era-nostalgia-tastes-like-froyo/); [Modern Retail](https://www.modernretail.co/marketing/whats-driving-frozen-yogurts-second-act-as-16-handles-and-go-greek-experience-a-renaissance/)). This shows up in occasion data too: Friday and Saturday now account for roughly **36% of weekly froyo visits**, more weekend-concentrated than ice cream, which Placer.ai frames as evidence froyo has become "a planned, social outing rather than an impulse stop, more akin to a coffee run than a drive-by treat" ([Placer.ai](https://www.placer.ai/anchor/articles/frozen-yogurts-comeback-is-more-than-nostalgia)). Go Greek Yogurt's London opening drew hour-long lines, with staff reporting customers "coming in multiple times a week," and design-forward NYC shops Myka, Mimi's, and Birdie's generate lines "around the block," with some patrons timing visits to get "froyo for breakfast" before crowds build ([Modern Retail](https://www.modernretail.co/marketing/whats-driving-frozen-yogurts-second-act-as-16-handles-and-go-greek-experience-a-renaissance/)). The strategic implication for a self-serve-heritage brand is pointed: reviving a toppings bar is unlikely, on current evidence, to capture this demand — the format consumers are responding to is curated and counter-served, a deliberate departure from (even as it trades nostalgically on) the earlier self-serve era.

## A Greek-yogurt wave and a GLP-1-driven protein-pint boom define new entrants

Excluding the brands already tracked elsewhere, the most significant new-concept activity is a "third wave" of small, founder-led, Greek-yogurt-based shops explicitly positioning froyo as premium and protein-forward. Spain's **Myka** (founded Madrid, 2023) has grown to 16–19+ countries and 200+ signed franchise contracts, opening its first US location in Miami's Lincoln Theatre in December 2025 using Greek yogurt imported weekly from Greece blended with "high-end kefir" ([Its Just Business Magazine](https://www.itsjustbusinessmagazine.com/myka-brings-cult-favorite-greek-yogurt-ice-cream-to-the-u-s-with-first-location-at-robert-rivanis-iconic-lincoln-theatre/)), and has since drawn direct imitators — Chicago's **Mikono** (a pop-up whose founder cites Myka as inspiration) and **Kouklas Greek Eatery** (568 units sold in July 2026 alone) — plus New Orleans' clean-label **Stelly's Froyo** and Brooklyn newcomers **Fira** and **Birdie's** ([WBEZ](https://www.wbez.org/food-drink/2026/08/18/froyo-greek-frozen-yogurt-trend-chicago-restaurants-mikono-kouklas-ema); [Greenpointers](https://greenpointers.com/2026/07/23/two-new-frozen-yogurt-shops-fira-and-birdies-opening-in-williamsburg/)). Notably, premium ice cream chain **Van Leeuwen** launched a permanent frozen yogurt line in March 2026, explicitly as a hedge against **GLP-1 (Ozempic-class) drugs** reshaping frozen-treat demand: overall US ice cream volume fell 1.5% YoY through mid-2026, but the super-premium segment was the only one still growing, as GLP-1 users cut frozen-treat consumption by at least 10% in volume while 90% still buy — trading down in quantity but up in quality ([eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/ice-cream-s-glp-1-adaptation-shows-opportunity-premium--portion-controlled-foods); [Inc.com](https://www.inc.com/mike-hofman/facing-glp-1s-van-leeuwen-ice-cream-betting-the-company-on-fro-yo/91340374)). The same GLP-1 logic is visible on grocery shelves: Magnum's **Yasso** launched its first-ever frozen Greek yogurt pint line at Target in October 2025 (up to 19g protein, ~400 calories per pint), upstart **Protein Pints** scaled from 14 Michigan stores (2024) to roughly 8,000 US retail doors by May 2025 across Kroger, Target, Albertsons, and Sprouts, and protein-bar brand **David Protein** sold out its frozen-dessert launch online in 28 minutes before rolling into Target nationwide at $7.99/pint ([Food Dive](https://www.fooddive.com/news/magnum-taps-into-better-for-you-craze-with-yasso-frozen-greek-yogurt-pints/802352/); [Refrigerated & Frozen Food](https://www.refrigeratedfrozenfood.com/articles/103586-protein-pints-expands-nationwide-with-target); [Dairy Foods](https://www.dairyfoods.com/articles/99176-david-protein-enters-frozen-dessert-category)). On marketing format, the standout 2025–2026 plays are artist/IP tie-ins with branded serveware (a JVKE "Red Velvet" collaboration ran September–October 2025 with branded spoons and music-note toppings) and a formal industry social-media leaderboard where a single-location LA shop, **The Bigg Chill**, was called out for outperforming large chains through daily short-form video and UGC reposting alone ([QSR Magazine](https://www.qsrmagazine.com/news/jvke-collaboration-brings-music-themed-frozen-yogurt-to-yogurtland/); [IFYA](https://internationalfrozenyogurt.com/news/the-frozen-yogurt-brands-winning-social-media-in-2026/)) — evidence that content cadence, not media budget, is now a competitive lever even small operators can win.

## Adjacent categories are growing faster than legacy froyo but not visibly cannibalizing it

Acai-bowl and boba chains are outgrowing legacy froyo on a chain-by-chain basis, even though no single source puts the two growth rates side by side. **Playa Bowls** grew from 163 locations three years ago to nearing/surpassing 400 by the end of 2025, with US systemwide sales rising from $296 million (2024) to **$332 million (2025)**; **Nekter Juice Bar** grew from 159 units (2022) to 202 (2025), a 27% three-year increase, though its growth rate is decelerating (11.3% → 9.0% → 4.7% YoY); **Vitality Bowls** grew from 65 to 71 units in the same year ([Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/buying-a-franchise/franchise-profile/how-playa-bowls-does-332-million-in-systemwide-sales); [QSR Magazine](https://www.qsrmagazine.com/news/nekter-juice-bar-plans-300-units-five-years/); [Franchise Times](https://www.franchisetimes.com/top-400-2025/457-vitality-bowls/article_02e20553-f422-494c-9725-8c9313619910.html)). US bubble tea market-size estimates vary two-fold by vendor ($531.69M to $1,075.9M for 2025), but CAGR projections cluster around **7–9.6%**, and Gong cha operates 240+ US stores after buying back 170 franchised locations to accelerate expansion ([IMARC Group](https://www.imarcgroup.com/united-states-bubble-tea-market); [Restaurant Dive](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/gong-cha-buys-170-us-master-franchise-stores/814179/)). Crucially, none of this reads as direct substitution: froyo foot traffic was itself accelerating through the same 2024–2026 window that acai and boba chains expanded, which argues these are parallel, co-existing growth stories rather than one visibly displacing the other — and no survey, spending-panel, or real-estate-leasing study was found that measures actual customer switching between categories. What is happening instead is menu convergence in both directions: Yogurtland and Menchie's have added acai bowls to core menus, Tropical Smoothie Cafe and Planet Smoothie have added boba-inspired items, and new entrant **Yo-Chi** (Australia's leading self-serve froyo brand, which opened its first US location in Dallas) was designed from inception as a combined froyo-and-acai concept — suggesting operators increasingly see these as complementary occasions to capture in one visit rather than adversarial categories to defend against ([Menchie's](https://www.menchies.com/froyo-toppings/acai-bowls/); [PRNewswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/its-poppin-tropical-smoothie-cafe-introduces-new-tropiboba-bowls-with-popping-boba-302703148.html)). Soft serve reads as a genuinely separate market: Dairy Queen's US footprint actually contracted by 97 units in 2025 even as froyo traffic grew, and froyo's weekend-concentrated, planned-outing visit pattern contrasts with soft serve's impulse/convenience occasion — the two appear to be differentiating on occasion type rather than competing head-on ([Franchise Times](https://www.franchisetimes.com/top-400-2025/18-dairy-queen/article_517c78b9-34d5-4728-a9af-d4d7887fc850.html)).

## Korea's Yoajung is the clearest US-Korea divergence, and it is exporting itself

Korea's frozen-dessert history is a sequence of fad-driven boom-bust cycles rather than one continuous category narrative, and its current cycle is running in the opposite direction from the US's legacy decline. The 빙수(shaved-ice)/**Sulbing** boom collapsed hard — revenue fell from KRW 20.2 billion (2014) to KRW 9.0 billion within about three years, driven by seasonality, store saturation, and a Korean Fair Trade Commission corrective order — before partially recovering to KRW 28.1 billion by 2024 ([Hankook Ilbo](https://www.hankookilbo.com/news/article/201606011593448555); [Saramin](https://www.saramin.co.kr/zf_user/company-info/view-inner-finance/csn/bEMrUzIzek1QVDFJajljZ3FUb3M5QT09/company_nm/(%EC%A3%BC)%EC%84%A4%EB%B9%99)). The genuine analogue to a US-style frozen-yogurt boom is **요아정(Yoajung)**, whose revenue rose from KRW 47.1 billion (2024) to **KRW 59.75 billion (2025), up 26.8% YoY**, with its offline store count exploding from 15 (2023) to 374 (2024) and operating profit reportedly growing roughly 40x in the year after its acquisition by Samhwa ([Meatlovernews](https://www.meatlovernews.co.kr/news/articleView.html?idxno=10452); [Nate News](https://news.nate.com/view/20250517n08840)). Yoajung is now the world's largest froyo chain by unit count (684 locations, +120 in 2025) and is actively expanding into Australia, Hong Kong, China, and already operates in the US, Singapore, Japan, Taiwan, and Vietnam — making it simultaneously Korea's clearest growth story and a direct, named international competitor already present in the same US market Yogurtland operates in ([FinanceToday](https://www.fntoday.co.kr/news/articleView.html?idxno=343085); [IFYA](https://internationalfrozenyogurt.com/news/the-froyo-year-in-review-2025-edition/)). Korean trade press itself flags the risk that this is a repeat of the country's rapid-fad-dessert pattern — explicitly comparing Yoajung's trajectory to the 2023 탕후루(candied-fruit) boom that subsequently busted — so its durability should be treated as a live, unresolved question rather than a settled trend ([E-science](https://www.e-science.co.kr/news/articleView.html?idxno=126286)). This Korea-specific story sits inside a broader, consistent global pattern: North America holds roughly 38–42% of global frozen yogurt revenue but is the slowest-growing major region (sub-4% CAGR), while Asia-Pacific is forecast at **10.5–11.25% CAGR**, with India cited as the fastest-growing major national market (12.31% CAGR) on organized-retail expansion and rising tier-2-city incomes ([Mordor Intelligence](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/asia-pacific-frozen-yogurt-market)). Value-consciousness is also reshaping format in Korea in a way the US hasn't documented: rising bingsu prices have pushed consumers toward smaller "cup bingsu" formats, with Sulbing's cup-format sales up 35% and delivery share up 18 points — a format-down, not demand-down, response to inflation that the US data doesn't show an equivalent for.

## Conclusion

The single most strategically useful reframing from this research is that "frozen yogurt is declining" and "frozen yogurt is reviving" are both true simultaneously in the US, because they describe different formats, not the same category at different moments. Retail scan data confirms the self-serve, pay-by-weight model that defined the 2000s–2010s boom is still losing dollars and units; foot-traffic and new-opening data confirm a parallel, counter-served, Greek-yogurt-based, protein-forward, premium-priced format is winning Gen Z attention, lines, and social virality. A Yogurtland marketer should treat this less as a category tailwind to ride passively and more as a format choice to make deliberately — and the choice is complicated by the fact that Korea's Yoajung has already proven the counter-service/health-adjacent model at massive scale and is actively exporting it into the same US market, while CPG players (Yasso, Protein Pints, David Protein) are capturing the high-protein positioning on grocery shelves faster than restaurant chains can on menus. The GLP-1 dynamic compounds this: if premium, portion-controlled, higher-protein frozen treats are structurally gaining share of a shrinking overall frozen-dessert occasion, the brands winning next are the ones that credibly own "protein-forward and premium" rather than "diet food" or "generic health halo" — a positioning lane the research suggests is still open, contested mainly by small independents and new entrants rather than by any of the legacy chains already being tracked.
